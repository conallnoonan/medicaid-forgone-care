Did Medicaid expansion reduce cost-related forgone care among low-income adults?

A policy evaluation using BRFSS survey data, comparing expansion and non-expansion states before and after expansion. Final project, graduate intro to applied social statistics (R).

Public health policySurvey data, complex designLogistic regression and difference-in-differences

Homework 5 answers

1. Problem

Did states that expanded Medicaid under the ACA see a larger drop in the share of low-income adults who skipped needed medical care because of cost, compared with states that did not expand?

Why it matters: Coverage expansions are judged by whether they change access to care, and Medicaid eligibility remains a live policy debate. The question has a clear treatment, a clear comparison group, and national data.

2. Underlying process

  • Expansion extends eligibility to adults up to 138% of the federal poverty line.
  • Eligible adults enroll, which lowers or removes out-of-pocket costs.
  • Lower costs raise the chance that someone seeks care when they need it, so fewer report skipping care because of cost.

Individual factors (income, age, health status, employment) shape exposure. State factors (provider supply, outreach, existing safety nets) shape how strongly the policy works. States chose whether to expand, so exposure is not randomly assigned. That makes confounding the central design problem.

3. Data to test the hypothesis

BRFSS is the primary source, with KFF expansion dates and state-level covariates from the ACS. Details are in the data sources table.

4. Dependent and primary independent variables

  • Dependent: whether the respondent needed a doctor in the past 12 months but could not afford one (BRFSS cost-barrier item). Secondary option: the weighted state-year share reporting a cost barrier.
  • Primary independent: expansion state × post-expansion period (the difference-in-differences term). A simpler baseline is a binary indicator for whether the respondent’s state had expanded at the time of the survey.
  • Controls: age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, income category, employment, plus state and year fixed effects.

6. Distribution of the data generating process

At the individual level the outcome is Bernoulli: each person either forgoes care or does not, with probability p that depends on covariates. Aggregated to state-year cells, the count out of n respondents is binomial. Real data will probably show overdispersion from unmeasured state differences, so the plan checks for it and falls back to quasibinomial standard errors or state-clustered errors. The logit link models p directly and keeps predictions between 0 and 1. Survey weights are required because BRFSS is a complex design.

7. Visualization

  • Distribution: bar chart of the weighted cost-barrier share by expansion status (before and after).
  • Relationship: line plot of the weighted cost-barrier rate by year, one line per group, with a vertical line at the expansion date. This is the figure in the header, built from real data.
  • Model results: coefficient or odds-ratio plot comparing models with and without controls.
  • Optional: state map of the change in cost-barrier rate, pre versus post.

Sample and variables

Proposed sample

  • Population: adults ages 19–64 (Medicaid expansion targets non-elderly adults).
  • Income restriction: the group most exposed to expansion. BRFSS reports income in categories, so use the category that best approximates under about 138% of poverty and say that it is a proxy.
  • Years: roughly 2011–2019. BRFSS changed its weighting method and added cell-phone samples in 2011, so earlier years are not comparable, and 2020 onward brings pandemic disruption and further changes.

Analysis plan

Descriptive stage

  • Weighted summary table of sample characteristics by expansion status.
  • Weighted cost-barrier rates by group and year; check pre-trends visually.

Primary model

Survey-weighted logistic regression, difference-in-differences form:

logit P(cost_barrier = 1) = b0 + b1(expansion_state × post) + X'g + state FE + year FE

Report the odds ratio on the interaction and also average marginal effects, which are easier to interpret in percentage points.

Robustness

  • Linear probability model with state-clustered standard errors, as a check on the logit.
  • Placebo test: higher-income adults (not eligible) should show little or no effect.
  • Mechanism check: repeat with “has any health coverage” as the outcome, since coverage is the proposed channel.
  • Event-study version with leads and lags to test the parallel trends assumption.
  • Extension, if time allows: staggered adoption makes simple two-way fixed effects unreliable, so compare against the Callaway–Sant’Anna estimator (did package).

Starter code

library(tidyverse); library(haven); library(survey); library(srvyr)

brfss <- read_xpt("data/raw/LLCP2015.XPT") |>
  transmute(state = `_STATE`, wt = `_LLCPWT`, strata = `_STSTR`, psu = `_PSU`,
            year = 2015,
            cost_barrier = case_when(MEDCOST == 1 ~ 1, MEDCOST == 2 ~ 0))

# Weighted rate by group
brfss |> filter(!is.na(cost_barrier)) |>
  as_survey_design(ids = psu, strata = strata, weights = wt, nest = TRUE) |>
  group_by(year) |>
  summarise(rate = survey_mean(cost_barrier, vartype = "ci"))

Repeat for each year, stack with bind_rows(), then join the expansion table on state.
